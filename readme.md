Currently, machine learning algorithms are used for signal classification and fingerprint recognition in radio frequency signals. Due to the presence of nonlinearities and other factors during manufacturing, each jammer has unique signal characteristics. However, even within the same location, the recording environment at different times is not entirely consistent. Therefore, modeling the impact of the environment on signal classification is crucial.  
To analyze these impacts, this study adopts a baseline Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architecture to classify different jammers based on the signals transmitted by the jammer. Additionally, a Bayesian LSTM method is introduced to model the uncertainty in predictions (i.e., the model's confidence). Researchers collected datasets in anechoic chambers using various radio frequency front-end devices, training and testing two networks under two scenarios: only transmitting jamming signals and combining outdoor antenna-forwarded GNSS signals.  
Understanding the environmental impact is the first step in developing robust fingerprint recognition methods. The main contribution of this study lies in the analysis of experimental results, which can support subsequent research (i.e., developing domain-adaptive algorithms to overcome these impacts and achieve generalization of the method).  
Due to the nonlinearity, variability, and manufacturing tolerances of radio equipment components, each radio device has its unique signal pattern. These patterns can be used to associate the source device with the signal. Moreover, the signal receiver and its antenna may add their own "fingerprint" to the signal. In real-world scenarios, jamming signals are typically recorded outdoors, meaning they will mix with GNSS signals and possibly other interference signals. At the same time, generally, jammers are located inside vehicles or there may be obstacles between the jammer and the receiver, which can attenuate the power level of the transmitted interference signal, leading to changes in the received signal characteristics.
The core concept of radio frequency fingerprinting is that each radio signal transmitter and receiver possess unique characteristics that can be extracted as fingerprints. The unique signal patterns of jammers originate from specific encoding, modulation methods, and hardware defects. Additionally, the environment and equipment used to record signals can also alter the signal patterns to some extent.
Currently, mainstream deep learning architectures for signal classification and fingerprint recognition are based on convolutional neural networks (CNN) and support vector machines (SVM). Although most related literature discusses fingerprint recognition for devices transmitting WiFi signals, there are also studies focusing on GNSS radio frequency interference (RFI) and type identification of jammers. For example, research has used SVM for the classification of GNSS interference signals, while others have presented signals through spectral imagery and used both SVM and CNN classifiers for type identification of jammers.
CNN is commonly used for feature extraction, with its input data being a concatenation of signal graphic representations, followed by classification using other machine learning techniques such as SVM; there are also studies that use CNN for detecting and classifying different types of signals based on spectral imagery input data. Moreover, there are proposals for extracting transmitter features using ResNet18-based deep learning architectures with wavelet scattering networks, and then classifying them using CNN; another scholar developed a deformable CNN to identify transmitters using different time-frequency and spectral representations.
SVM has high memory efficiency and performs well on high-dimensional data, but it struggles to construct decision boundaries (i.e., determine the category of samples) when the data is noisy. CNN's strength lies in its strong feature extraction capabilities and high classification accuracy, but converting signals into input images is often time-consuming.
Overall, CNNs leverage the spatial characteristics of input data, while signal features vary over time, making temporal patterns crucial. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks are designed to exploit the temporal characteristics of input data. Previous studies have employed LSTM and Convolutional LSTM architectures for classification of transmitters based on signal features. Due to the susceptibility of deep learning models to overfitting when training data is small, this study aims to compare traditional LSTM with Bayesian LSTM methods. The Bayesian Deep Learning (BDL) model can reduce model overfitting, and for the Bayesian approach, researchers not only focus on the accuracy of the model but also evaluate the reliability of the prediction output (through prediction entropy assessment).
The ultimate goal of this study is to develop a method for extracting unique fingerprints from recorded signals to prevent the use of unauthorized devices. To achieve a generalizable method that can identify device fingerprints that do not appear in the training data of machine learning models, it is necessary to separate clearly defined device signal characteristics. Researchers believe that signal type and environmental features play a dominant role in the received signal, so these should be eliminated before seeking the fingerprints.
Based on this, this study developed an interferencer classification method and analyzed the impact of environmental features on prediction results. To achieve generalizability, the method needs to be interpretable (clearly identifying which features constitute the unique fingerprint of the signal) and reliable. To compare the applicability of deep learning in this task, researchers implemented traditional Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Bayesian Long Short-Term Memory (Bayesian LSTM), which will be introduced first in the following sections. Following this introduction, detailed discussions on experimental results will be provided, followed by conclusions.
IV. Method Introduction 1. Model Architecture The existing mainstream radio frequency fingerprint recognition and identification methods mainly adopt two machine learning models: CNN or SVM. CNN has been proven to be applicable for image classification. However, it is more inclined to capture spatial information; Recurrent deep learning models, such as LSTMs, are capable of capturing the temporal dynamics of input data. This study developed a model based on LSTM. To analyze the impact of environmental factors on the classification accuracy of jammers. At the same time, By using two different RF front-end devices, Testing the impact of signal recording devices on classification results (LSTM will also be used for the development of future actual fingerprint recognition algorithms). Each LSTM layer contains multiple LSTM units. These units receive input data, And utilize the hidden states and cell states learned from the previous unit. Calculate the hidden state and cell state of the current time step.
The difference between the Bayesian LSTM architecture and traditional LSTM lies in the representation of network layer parameters: whereas the parameters of traditional LSTM are point estimates (i.e., single values), the parameters θ in the Bayesian LSTM are treated as a distribution. In this study's architecture, θ follows a Gaussian distribution, with each parameter unit represented as θ = (μ, ρ), where μ and ρ represent the mean and standard deviation parameters, respectively.
2. Bayesian Deep Learning Core Principles: The main goal of Bayesian Deep Learning (BDL) is to quantify the posterior uncertainty of a Bayesian model. Theoretically, The posterior distribution p(w|D) of the network weight w must be first found. The distribution describes the relationship between data D and weights w through Bayesian formula. Subsequently, By integrating over all possible weight values. The uncertainty in predicting y when given the input x and data D. However, due to the high dimensionality of the parameter space. The distribution of p(w|D) is extremely difficult or even infeasible to calculate.
Therefore, the problem is addressed using variational methods (such as the expectation-lower bound method), which involves first approximating the posterior distribution of network parameters and then minimizing the difference between the approximated distribution q_θ(w) and the posterior distribution p(w|D) via KL divergence. The objective is reformulated through formula reconstruction, combining the KL divergence with an expectation term to construct a loss function for each minibatch data.
Bayesian Backpropagation (Bayes by Backprop) algorithm is similar to traditional deep learning backpropagation algorithms, but before calculating the forward and backward propagations, it requires sampling the approximate distribution and computing the KL divergence loss. This loss is used for optimization steps, updating the mean μ and standard deviation parameter ρ through gradient descent. The classification output is determined by the Softmax activation function, and the model's prediction uncertainty is obtained through multiple prediction sampling of each input sequence, with the final prediction result for the interferer category being the mode of the sampled predictions.
3. Data and Training Settings: (1) Data Collection Research collected a dataset of 21 linear frequency modulation signal interferers in a soundproof room (divided into 8 categories according to the manufacturer and type reported), divided into two types: containing GNSS signals and not containing GNSS signals, collected at Trondheim, Norway, and Helsinki, Finland.
The collection equipment included two USRP B210 software-defined radios and one ARFIDAAS radio frequency front-end device. The experiment in Helsinki utilized rooftop Novatel GNSS-850 antennas for receiving GNSS signals and relaying them within the soundproof room using multiple GNSS repeaters; the USRP devices used Trimble Zephyr 3 Rover antennas, while the ARFIDAAS device used TaoGlas Magma X2 antennas. Pure interferer signals were collected in two ways: directly connected to the front-end device via cables or air-transmitted signals from integrated antennas of the interferer. All signals were only considered single frequencies in the L-band.
(2) Data Processing: The raw I/Q data obtained from the RF front-end is converted from time domain to frequency domain to reduce the size of the model input vector. The power spectral density (PSD) is calculated using the Welch method (combined with Hanning window function) in the SciPy signal library, and then transformed into v_i = 10·log₁₀(|P_i|) (where P_i is the PSD of the i-th 4 ms long signal segment), followed by normalization to the range [0,1] through the formula v_i=10·log₁₀(|P_i|) to accelerate model convergence. The final input of the neural network is a sequence of four consecutive v_i vectors, each from the same signal.
(3) Training parameters: The model is implemented based on the PyTorch library, and the Bayesian layer uses the PyTorch BLiTZ library for modeling. The traditional LSTM model consists of two layers of LSTM and one linear layer, followed by a Softmax activation function, using cross-entropy loss function; the loss function of the Bayesian LSTM model is a combination of KL divergence and an expectation term. Both models use the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001.
The first hidden state size of the traditional LSTM is 512, the second layer is 256, and the input feature number of the linear layer is 128; corresponding parameters for the Bayesian LSTM are 512, 70, and 30 respectively. The output vector length is 21 (corresponding to 21 interference categories), each LSTM layer contains 4 LSTM units (consistent with the length of the input sequence), and the training batch size is 128.
The prior of the Bayesian network is a mixture of two Gaussian distributions, and the standard deviations of all layers are set to σ₁=1.25 and σ₂=0.0002, and the prior scaling coefficients π for both layers are set to 0.9; the initial values of the posterior ρ for the first and second layers of LSTM are -9 and -3.5 respectively (standard deviation σ=log(1+exp(ρ))>0). The dataset is divided into 80% training, 5% validation, and 15% testing according to the ratio of 80%, 5%, and 15%.
5. Conclusions This study employs two deep learning model architectures: traditional LSTM and Bayesian LSTM, to evaluate the impact of different data collection methods on prediction capabilities. Understanding the various characteristics of data collection (environment, equipment, signal types) is the first step in developing robust interference fingerprint recognition and identification models.
The research found that some interferencers from the same manufacturer exhibit significant differences in signal shape and central frequency, making them easy to distinguish, while others are difficult to differentiate. Therefore, networks that indicate model prediction uncertainty (such as Bayesian LSTM) are of great significance. Additionally, if data deviates (e.g., noisy data or bandwidth differences between training and testing data), Bayesian networks also struggle to accurately identify categories.
Experimental results show that when using the same type of equipment to record data (including pure interference signals and mixed interference signals with GNSS), there is no significant difference between the training phase and the testing phase; however, when the training data and testing data differ in signal type (pure interference signal vs mixed GNSS signal) or radio frequency front-end equipment (USRP vs ARFIDAAS), the model classification accuracy significantly drops (down to less than 5%). This is mainly due to the characteristic differences in radio frequency front-end equipment (e.g., bandwidth, quantization depth, automatic gain control) and environmental factors (e.g., antenna setup, interferencer placement location, changes in signal shape due to device shell pressing).
Although this classification method is suitable for demonstrating the impact of environment and equipment, it does not apply to interferencer fingerprint recognition (as a category can be considered independent at the device level). Therefore, future research will develop deep learning techniques to first eliminate dominant signal features (including interference signal type, collection environment, and receiver influence), then extract individual interferencer fingerprints from the data.
At the same time, domain adaptation methods will be developed to adapt to the data distribution differences caused by changes in environment and receivers; deep learning methods will be based on Bayesian models to find latent distributions under limited and sparse training data and provide prediction confidence information.
